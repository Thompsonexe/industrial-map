Speaking script: Hong Kong's Industrial Land
The main idea to keep coming back to: Factories left Hong Kong decades ago, but the land is still zoned for them. What's stopping that land from being reused, and what's starting to move in?

Every slide should answer the question the slide before it raised. The → lines at the end of each slide are your bridges, and they're what make the talk flow.

Slide 1: Opening (map of all industrial zones)
Everything you see lit up on this map is land Hong Kong has set aside for industry. Orange, yellow, purple, blue: that's roughly [total] hectares.

Here's the strange part. Hong Kong barely makes anything anymore. So why do we still have this much industrial land, and what's actually happening on it?

→ "To answer that, we need to know what these colours mean."

Slide 2: What counts as "industrial" (zone stats)
Planners split this land into four types.
Industrial, the orange, is traditional factory land: [X] ha.
Industrial Group D, the yellow, is mostly in the New Territories and allows lighter, rural industry: [X] ha.
OU(B), the purple, is "Other Specified Uses – Business". These are old industrial areas that have already been opened up to offices and shops: [X] ha.
And Open Storage, the blue, which is containers, car parks and building materials.

Altogether that's about [share]% of all zoned land.

→ "Most of this was zoned when Hong Kong was a factory city. So what changed?"

Slide 3: The factories left (Greater Bay Area map)
From the 1980s on, manufacturers moved across the border to Shenzhen, Dongguan, Huizhou and Zhuhai, where land and labour were far cheaper.

Hong Kong's role changed. Instead of making goods, it imports the finished products, and handles the trading, finance and shipping around them.

But the factory buildings stayed behind, and so did the zoning.

→ "So what happened to those buildings?"

Slide 4: What's left behind (vacancy and rents)
Many old industrial buildings now sit partly empty or are used for things they were never designed for: storage, small offices, even illegal subdivided flats.

According to the Rating and Valuation Department, flatted factory vacancy is around [X]%. Knight Frank reports rents have [fallen / been flat] through 2025 and 2026.

The space isn't worthless, but it's in the wrong form for today's economy.

→ "The government's first answer was to rezone the land."

Slide 5: Rezoning to OU(B) (purple highlighted)
This purple land is where the government has already rezoned old industrial areas to OU(B). Owners can now redevelop into offices, shops or mixed business uses.

The idea was simple: change the rules and the market will rebuild.

→ "The best place to see whether that worked is Kowloon East."

Slide 6: Kowloon East case study (zoom into Kwun Tong / Kowloon Bay)
Kwun Tong and Kowloon Bay were Hong Kong's factory heartland. Today they're the city's second business district: glass office towers standing right next to 1960s factory blocks.

Look closely and you'll see it's patchy. Some lots were redeveloped, and the neighbouring lot is unchanged after 50 years.

→ "If the zoning allows it, why hasn't every building been redeveloped? There are two big barriers. The first is money."

Slide 7: Barrier 1, the land premium (calculator)
In Hong Kong, the government owns the land. To change a building from industrial to commercial, the owner has to pay a land premium: the increase in land value.

Since 2023, Lands Department has used standard rates to make this predictable. Here's an example in Kowloon East:

A 10,000 m² industrial building.
Switching to commercial means a premium of about HK$105 million.
If the owner also gets a 20% bonus in floor area, it rises to about HK$177 million.
(Change the numbers live if you're showing the calculator.)

That's a big bill before a single brick is laid.

→ "Even if an owner can pay, there's a second problem: who actually owns the building?"

Slide 8: Barrier 2, fragmented ownership (Cap. 545)
Many old industrial buildings were sold off floor by floor, or even unit by unit. One building can have dozens of owners.

To redevelop, a developer needs to buy them all out. The Compulsory Sale Ordinance (Cap. 545) lets a developer who holds a large majority force a sale of the rest. That threshold has been lowered for older buildings [put the exact % from your slide here].

Even so, assembling a whole site is slow, expensive and often ends up in court.

→ "So redevelopment is hard. But some industrial land is finding new jobs, and they're not what you'd expect."

Slide 9: New use 1, construction logistics (MiC route map)
Hong Kong is building more and more with MiC, Modular Integrated Construction. Whole rooms are built in factories and assembled on site.

Follow the route: the modules are ① made in Huizhou, ② shipped into the River Trade Terminal in Tuen Mun, then ③ trucked to sites like Tseung Kwan O.

Every step needs storage and staging space, which is exactly what industrial and open storage land provides.

→ "Construction is one new use. The other one is a much bigger bet."

Slide 10: New use 2, the counter-trend (data centres and cold storage)
Some global investors are buying into industrial land:

Fanling: Amazon Web Services bought data centres for about HK$2.45 billion.
Tsing Yi: Brookfield paid about HK$663 million for cold storage.
Data centres and cold chains need big floor plates, heavy floor loading and reliable power, and old industrial buildings have all three.

Industrial land isn't dying. It's changing what it's for.

→ "And the government is betting on that shift, especially in the north."

Slide 11: Northern Metropolis (New Territories view)
The 2025 Policy Address puts the Northern Metropolis at the centre of Hong Kong's plans, with new land set aside for innovation and technology, right next to Shenzhen.

It's a full circle. Forty years ago, industry crossed the border northward. Now Hong Kong is trying to build a new kind of industry facing that same border.

→ "So what does all this tell us?"

Slide 12: Conclusion
Three takeaways:

The land outlived the factories. Hong Kong still has hundreds of hectares zoned for an economy that moved away.
Changing the rules wasn't enough. Land premiums and fragmented ownership keep many old buildings stuck.
New demand is showing up: data centres, cold storage, construction logistics and I&T. The question is whether policy can keep up.
The colours on this map haven't changed much in 40 years. What happens on the land is changing fast.

→ "Thank you. My sources are on the last slide, and I'm happy to take questions."

Why this order flows better
Problem, then barriers, then what's next: each section ends on a question the next one answers.
The barriers come in a pair ("the first is money… the second is ownership"), which is easy for listeners to follow.
The ending connects back to the start (the border and the map colours), so the talk feels finished.
At a normal pace it runs about 8 to 10 minutes. To cut time, merge slides 4 and 5, or slides 10 and 11.


What this slide is about
The slide makes one point: Hong Kong has very little industrial land, but it still has more factory floor space than anyone wants to use.

The title: "Little land, but still too much space"
There are two kinds of supply:

Land supply is small. Hong Kong zones very little new land for industry.
Floor space supply is too big. The existing buildings (flatted factories, which are multi-storey buildings split into many factory units) have more space than tenants need.
So the problem isn't a shortage. The space that exists isn't being used.

What each number means
Number	Meaning
7.6%	Share of all flatted factory space that was empty at the end of 2025. It rose from 7.0% in 2024, so things got worse.
1.23M m²	That 7.6% as an actual area: about 1.23 million m² of empty factory floors.
79,000 m²	New flatted factory space expected to be finished in 2026.
123,000 m²	New space expected in 2027, which is even more.
The takeaway: empty space is already going up, yet more new space keeps being built. Unless demand recovers, vacancy will probably keep rising.

Your numbers check out
They match the government's Rating and Valuation Department (RVD), Hong Kong Property Review 2026. The same report has more facts you could use to back up the slide:

Take-up was negative (−114,000 m²) in 2025. More space was emptied than filled, so demand actually shrank. It was also negative in 2024 (−212,000 m²).
Prices fell 15.2% from late 2024 to late 2025, and rents fell 3.0%. Both are signs of weak demand.
Half of the empty space is in Kwun Tong, Tsuen Wan and Kwai Tsing. This connects well to your Kwun Tong slide.
New space in 2026 will mostly come from Sha Tin, Sai Kung and Kwai Tsing. New space in 2027 will all come from Kwai Tsing and Sai Kung.
